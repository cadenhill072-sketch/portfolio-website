Eagle Scout service project — 2018–2019

Building a period game set for a heritage house

A local historic house museum wanted something visitors could actually touch. I researched, designed, sourced and hand-built a set of period-appropriate games, including steam-bent wooden hoops made on a form I built myself.

The ask

The house interprets [period] life for school groups and visitors, and wanted a hands-on element — something period-accurate that kids could pick up and play with on the lawn rather than look at behind a rope. That was the whole brief. Everything past that was mine to define.

So the first month was not building anything. It was research into what games were actually played in the period, then a presentation to the house’s staff to agree on what I would make before I bought a single board.

Design and sourcing

I modeled the pieces in SketchUp before committing to dimensions, which mattered more than I expected: the hoops had to be a size a child could actually catch, and the storage box had to hold everything without the hoops warping against each other. Drawings went through a couple of rounds with my project coach.

Materials came from a hardware-store bill of materials I priced out line by line, then partly covered through donation requests. Asking local businesses to donate against a specific, costed list is a very different conversation than asking for help, and it is the first time I understood that a good sourcing ask is mostly preparation.

Steam-bending is unforgiving. The wood tells you within seconds whether you prepared correctly.

The hard part: bending wood

The hoops are steam-bent. A 37-inch strip goes into a soak, then into steam, then onto a form and into clamps — and the window where it will bend without cracking is short. My first session ran nearly four hours between soaking and clamping, most of it waiting and then moving very fast.

What made it work was building the form first and doing a test piece before committing the good stock. What made it slow was learning that lesson on the second attempt rather than the first.

Running the work session

The group build day is the part an Eagle project is really testing. Four hours of prep went in beforehand: cutting and processing stock so that volunteers arrived to defined tasks rather than to a pile of lumber and a question. On the day I had people at different skill levels working in parallel, which meant sequencing the jobs so nobody was blocked waiting on me.

That is the piece I carried into supply chain work. A build day fails the same way a project plan fails — not because people are unwilling, but because the work in front of them was never broken down small enough to hand off.

What I’d do differently

Two things. I would build the bending form and run a scrap test before the first real piece rather than after, which would have saved most of a session. And I would have written the game instructions early instead of at the end — writing them forced decisions about the pieces that I would rather have made while I could still change the design.
