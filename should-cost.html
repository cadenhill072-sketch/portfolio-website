Machined Bracket Should-Cost Model

What a machined aluminum bracket should cost a fair supplier to make and deliver, and how to check a price increase blamed on aluminum.

Built from public market data. The part, the supplier quote and the price increase are made up, and there's no employer or supplier data in it.

Why I built it

A question comes up all the time in sourcing: how do you know whether a supplier's price is actually fair? A should-cost model works backwards from what goes into a part and what it takes to make it, so you walk into a negotiation with your own number instead of just reacting to the quote.

I picked a machined aluminum part because that kind of part shows up everywhere in aerospace, defense and auto supply chains, and because the 2025 tariffs made aluminum one of the most talked about costs in manufacturing.

How it works

The part is a made-up actuator mounting bracket, about 6 x 3 x 2 inches, machined from 6061-T6511 bar on a 3-axis machining center and then clear anodized.

The model starts with the metal: the LME aluminum price plus the US Midwest premium, plus what it costs to turn that into a 6061 bar and get it cut and delivered. About 3.715 lb of bar goes into each part and only 1.18 lb ships, so the other 2.535 lb comes back as chips that the shop sells for scrap.

Then it builds an hourly machine rate from scratch, using depreciation, maintenance, power, floor space, and national operator and machinist wages from BLS with benefits added. The run rate comes to $53.97 an hour at cost and $66.49 once the supplier's overhead and profit are on it, which lands inside the range shops publish for 3-axis work, so the machining part of the number holds up. Each part takes 11.8 minutes on the machine.

After that it adds setup, deburr, tooling, inspection, anodize, packing, freight, and the supplier's overhead and profit. Every input sits on one tab with a note on where it came from.

What one bracket should cost

With November's aluminum prices the bracket should cost about $34.20 delivered. The bar is only about 40% of the price, shown in red below. The chip credit comes off the total, since the shop sells the scrap.

Against the quote

The made-up quote I tested against was $38.90, so it's $4.697 a part over should-cost, or about 12% high. On 12,000 parts a year, closing half of that gap gets the price to $36.5515 and saves about $28,182 a year. Getting all the way to should-cost would be worth $56,364.

The model also writes the price out as a contract formula. You agree on the conversion price once and let the metal part move with the market:

(LME cash + Midwest premium + $1.16/lb) x 3.715 lb + $20.41 per part

The price increase check

This is the tab I'd use most. The Midwest price of aluminum (LME plus the premium) went from $2.1692 a pound in November 2025 to $2.5534 in November 2026, up about 18%.

Say a supplier puts a flat 9% on the whole part price because of that. On a made-up current price of $38.90, that's $42.401. Aluminum only justifies about 3.2%, or $1.2638 a part, because machining, anodize and margin didn't move with the metal and the chips are worth more now too. The difference is about $2.24 a part, or roughly $26,847 a year.

What moves the price

The two things that move this part most are the Midwest premium and how long the part sits on the machine.

Move the premium and the cycle time. The numbers come straight from the workbook's sensitivity tab.
